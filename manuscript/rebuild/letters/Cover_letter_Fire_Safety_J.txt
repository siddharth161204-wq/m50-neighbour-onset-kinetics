Dear Professor Bisby and Professor Merci,

I submit the manuscript "Thermal runaway propagation between two lithium-ion cells: resolved and lumped neighbour models against sixteen tests" for consideration as a research article in the Fire Safety Journal.

Models of thermal runaway propagation in modules must choose how much of a failing cell's vented heat reaches its neighbour, which published tests do not measure, and must represent each cell as one temperature or as a resolved temperature field. The manuscript asks how much heat must reach a neighbour for it to follow, and whether published tests can say, for two touching LG M50 cells modelled with kinetics fitted to calorimetry of NCM811 and graphite, against sixteen published two-cell tests.

Each failing cell's energy is fitted to its own measured peak temperature, 31.6 to 54.3 kJ in fourteen tests (strong triggers) and 9.0 and 16.6 kJ in two (weak), and the neighbour is solved both as one temperature and as a three-dimensional field; in each, the heat it needs through its side facing the failing cell to run away at the test's measured time is searched, and the bounds that published inputs set on each path that could deliver it are worked out.

From a failing cell releasing at most 33.2 kJ, the most that calorimetry measured for this cell, conduction and radiation leave the neighbour at least 29 K short of runaway. With heat delivered over 21.4 s, the mean of the two rise times read from the two published records, the eight tests that propagated after strong triggers need 4.45 to 7.54 kJ if the neighbour is resolved in three dimensions and 0.26 to 5.96 kJ if it is lumped, 5.8 to 9.8 % and 0.3 to 7.8 % of the Databank's median ejecta energy for this cell, 76.74 kJ. To run away at all, the resolved neighbour needs at least 1.36 times the lumped neighbour's energy after the four strong triggers searched in three dimensions and at most 0.91 times after both weak ones. Published inputs do not exclude any path delivering 27.9 kJ to the facing side within 21.4 s.

For readers who model propagation in packs, the paper gives the heat a neighbour needs under each of the two common representations of a cell, set against sixteen tests, the factor between them and its direction, and the measurement, a heat flux on the neighbour's facing side, that could test them.

It falls within fire safety science and engineering as a quantitative account of the heat that carries thermal runaway from one cell to the next.

The code, inputs and run records are archived openly, with the study's 98 predictions, each committed before its runs.

I am a mechanical engineer (B.Tech 2026, COEP Technological University, Pune), working independently, with no funding and no competing interests. The manuscript has not been published and is not under consideration elsewhere.
