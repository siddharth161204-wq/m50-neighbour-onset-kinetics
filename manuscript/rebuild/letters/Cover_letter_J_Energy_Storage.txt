I submit the manuscript "Thermal runaway propagation between lithium-ion cells: a lumped neighbour needs less heat than a resolved one after strong triggers" for consideration as a research article in the Journal of Energy Storage.

Models of thermal runaway propagation in modules treat each cell as one temperature or as a resolved field, and choose what share of the failing cell's vented heat reaches its neighbour. The manuscript asks how much delivered heat a neighbour needs under each treatment, for two touching LG M50 cells with decomposition kinetics published for another chemistry (NCM811 and graphite), against sixteen published two-cell tests.

It finds that the choice matters, in a direction set by the trigger. With each test's trigger fitted to its measured peak, the resolved neighbour needs 1.5 and 1.7 times the heat of the lumped neighbour in the two tests with strong triggers (fitted body energies of 31.6 to 54.3 kJ) where both thresholds are bracketed, and 0.85 times in the one with a weak trigger. A share of the ejected energy calibrated on such tests with one treatment therefore cannot be carried to the other without a correction whose sign depends on the trigger. Reduced models give the mechanisms: for pulses up to 120 s, conduction into the surface layer sets the resolved cell's hot spot, and an energy balance to an ignition temperature of 218.5 °C sets the lumped cell's runaway. The kinetics' published uncertainty moves that temperature by 6.1 K, while matching the two thresholds in one test would need a cell's reaction rates over 17.5 K hotter, and the chemistry mismatch lies outside that 6.1 K. At the central contact a two-dimensional reactive cross-section reproduced the outcome of all 151 three-dimensional runs it was checked against, at a fraction of their cost, making a resolved neighbour affordable in a module model.

In the model, with the failing cell's energy held to calorimetry, conduction and radiation leave the neighbour at least 29 K short of runaway, so the ejecta or other paths must supply the rest. Published inputs cannot bound any path through the neighbour's facing half below 27.9 kJ within 21.4 s, above what either treatment needs at the measured times, so the sixteen tests cannot decide between the treatments. The one published record of a propagating neighbour starts its final rise 37 K cooler than the resolved model's far side; a heat flux measured on the neighbour's facing side would decide.

It falls within the journal's topics of modelling and of the safety of energy storage systems: it gives what a neighbouring cell in a module needs before it follows a failing one.

The code, inputs and run records are openly archived, and the study's 98 predictions were committed before the runs they concern.
