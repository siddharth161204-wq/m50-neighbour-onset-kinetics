I submit the manuscript "Thermal runaway propagation between lithium-ion cells: a lumped neighbour needs less heat than a resolved one after strong triggers" for consideration as a research article in the Journal of Power Sources.

Models of thermal runaway propagation in lithium-ion modules treat each cell either as one temperature or as a resolved temperature field, and where they include the heat of the failing cell's vented ejecta they choose what share of it reaches the neighbour. The manuscript asks how much delivered heat a neighbour needs under each treatment, for two touching LG M50 cells with published decomposition kinetics, and sets both against sixteen published two-cell tests.

It finds that the choice matters, and in a direction that depends on the trigger. With each test's trigger fitted to its measured peak, the resolved neighbour needs 1.5 and 1.7 times the heat of the lumped neighbour in the two tests with strong triggers (fitted body energies of 31.6 to 54.3 kJ) where both thresholds are bracketed, and 0.85 times in the one with a weak trigger. A share of the ejected energy calibrated on such tests with one treatment therefore cannot be carried to the other without a correction whose sign depends on the trigger. Each threshold has a mechanism that a reduced model captures: for pulses up to 120 s, conduction into the surface layer sets the resolved cell's hot spot, and an energy balance to an ignition temperature of 218.5 °C sets the lumped cell's runaway. The published uncertainty of the kinetics moves that temperature by 6.1 K, while matching the two thresholds in one test would need reaction rates of a cell over 17.5 K hotter. At the central contact a reactive cross-section of the cell reproduced the runaway outcome of all 151 three-dimensional runs it was checked against, at a fraction of their cost, which makes a resolved neighbour affordable in a module model.

In the model, with the failing cell's energy limited to its calorimetry value, conduction and radiation leave the neighbour at least 29 K short of runaway, so the rest of the heat must come from the ejecta or other paths. Published inputs cannot bound any path through the neighbour's facing half below 27.9 kJ within 21.4 s, above what either treatment needs at the measured times, so the sixteen tests cannot decide between the treatments; the paper names the measurement that would, the heat flux on the neighbour's facing side.

It falls within the journal's priority areas of safety and of multiscale simulation set against experiments: it couples a cell's decomposition kinetics to its heat transfer and compares the result with measured propagation tests.

The code, inputs and run records are openly archived, and the study's 98 predictions were committed before the runs they concern.
