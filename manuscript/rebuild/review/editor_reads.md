# The editor's-eye reads (Brief 29, deliverable 5)

Each read was made by a separate agent that saw only the title, the abstract, the highlights and the Journal of Power Sources cover letter, as an editor screening for review, and answered in three sentences: what the paper is about, what it found, and whether it would go to review and why. Each agent was new and had not seen the earlier reads or the manuscript. The texts it saw are recorded with each read below.

## Read 1 (20 November 2026)

Seen: the title "The heat a neighbouring lithium-ion cell needs for thermal runaway propagation: resolved and lumped thresholds against sixteen two-cell tests", the first abstract (which opened "How much heat must reach a failing lithium-ion cell's neighbour for it to follow, and can published inputs say?" and stated that 42 of 98 pre-registered predictions failed), its highlights and its letter.

The read, verbatim:

> The paper models thermal runaway propagation between two touching LG M50 cells using published decomposition kinetics, representing the neighbouring cell either as a single lumped temperature or as a spatially resolved temperature field, and compares both against sixteen published two-cell tests to ask how much heat the neighbour must receive to follow the failing cell into runaway and whether published inputs can determine that. As far as I can decode an abstract that is mostly unexplained, over-precise numbers, it reports that 42 of its 98 pre-registered predictions failed, that with the failing cell's energy held to calorimetry, conduction and radiation leave the neighbour at least 29 K short of runaway, that the resolved neighbour needs about 7 kJ, 1.49 and 1.72 times the lumped neighbour's need in just two of the sixteen tests, that matching the thresholds (the abstract, highlights and cover letter each describe this match differently) would require shifting the kinetics by more than 17.5 K against a 6.1 K posterior span, and that because published inputs bound every delivery path at 27.9 kJ or more, above every need, they cannot decide between the two representations, which it leaves to a heat-flux measurement on the neighbour's facing side that it proposes but does not make. No, I would not send it to review: the topic is squarely within the journal's scope, but what decides it is that by its own account the paper cannot settle the quantity its title promises, so it offers mismatches with the tests, a ratio between two of the author's own model variants and an experiment for someone else to run, with its central mismatch, the conduction and radiation shortfall, never traced to what does deliver the heat or squared with every delivery path being bounded above every need, and nothing in the four items makes the case for more, since the failed predictions go unexplained, 'skin conduction', 'far face' and a 'posterior span' on kinetics described as published go undefined, the 255.7 °C runaway temperature is never related to the 218.5 °C ignition temperature, and the cover letter restates the abstract rather than saying what this adds to existing propagation models.

What changed after it: the abstract was rewritten to say where the missing heat must come from and why the bounds do not decide, to define the two thresholds in words (conduction into the surface layer; the ignition temperature where reaction heat outruns losses), to drop the count of failed predictions, and to give the ratios to the precision the claim needs; the highlights and the letter were made to state the kinetic comparison the same way; the letter was rewritten to say what the paper adds to propagation models.

## Read 2 (20 November 2026)

Seen: the title "The heat a neighbouring lithium-ion cell needs for thermal runaway propagation depends on how the cell is modelled", the second abstract, its highlights and its letter.

The read, verbatim:

> The paper is a computational study built entirely on published data: it models two touching LG M50 cells, using published decomposition kinetics, the failing cell's energy fixed by calorimetry and sixteen published two-cell tests as the benchmark, and asks how much delivered heat the neighbouring cell needs before it runs away when that cell is modelled as a single lumped temperature rather than as a resolved temperature field. It finds that conduction and radiation alone leave the neighbour at least 29 K short of runaway, so ejecta or other paths must supply the rest, and that the resolved neighbour needs about 7 kJ, which is 1.5 and 1.7 times what the lumped one needs (a ratio quoted for only two of the sixteen tests), a gap larger than the kinetic uncertainty can explain; but since published inputs let those paths deliver 27.9 kJ or more against needs of at most 8.1 kJ, neither the tests nor the inputs can say which treatment is right, the one published temperature history fits neither model (I could not parse the abstract's own sentence on it, about a jump "from 37 K below the resolved neighbour's far side"), and the paper ends by naming a facing-side heat-flux measurement that would decide. No, I would decline it without review: the topic is squarely within the journal's safety scope, but the deciding reason is that by its own account the paper neither tells the two treatments apart nor validates either, and never shows that the difference changes whether a module propagates or how it should be designed, so what remains is a demonstration for one pair of cells that two modelling choices give different numbers, close to the truism its title states, written in shorthand I had to decode line by line ("held to calorimetry", "every rate evaluated over 17.5 K hotter"), while the one result that could carry a paper, a reduced cross-section model said to reproduce the outcome of all 151 three-dimensional runs at a fraction of their cost, appears only in the cover letter and not in the abstract or highlights.

What changed after it: the title now states the direction of the difference and its dependence on the trigger ("a lumped neighbour needs less heat than a resolved one after strong triggers"); the abstract states the reversal after the weak trigger (0.85 times), puts the reactive cross-section in a sentence of its own, and rewrites the shorthand ("with the failing cell's energy set by calorimetry", "reactions running as if over 17.5 K hotter", "starts its runaway jump 37 K cooler than the resolved model's surface"); the highlights carry the cross-section; the letter says what the result means for module models (a lumped cell cannot stand in for a resolved one in either direction; the cross-section makes a resolved neighbour affordable); the conclusions carry the reversal and the cross-section.

## Read 4 (20 November 2026)

The structural reason, that the paper compares two models the published tests cannot separate, is the paper's design (the brief's rule that it does not say which model is right) and stands.

## Read 13 (23 November 2026)
