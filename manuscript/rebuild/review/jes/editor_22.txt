The paper asks how much heat a lithium-ion cell in thermal runaway must deliver to a touching neighbour for the neighbour to follow, and whether the answer changes when the neighbour is modelled as a single temperature (lumped) or as a three-dimensional temperature field (resolved), using NCM811/graphite decomposition kinetics to model sixteen published propagation tests on pairs of LG M50 cells. It finds that conduction and radiation alone run no resolved neighbour away, whether each trigger's energy is fitted to its measured peak temperature, which mostly gives values above the 33.2 kJ calorimetric maximum, or held to that maximum; that the eight propagating strong-trigger tests need 4.45 to 7.54 kJ delivered to the facing side over 21.4 s if the neighbour is resolved but 0.26 to 5.96 kJ if lumped; and that at nominal inputs the resolved threshold is at least 1.36 times the lumped one after the four strong triggers searched in three dimensions but at most 0.91 times after both weak ones, from which it infers that a vent-heat share calibrated in a lumped model may need a correction whose direction depends on the trigger's energy, while conceding that published inputs rule out no path delivering as much as 27.9 kJ in that time. I would send it to review, though narrowly: what decides it is that the work sits squarely in the journal's modelling and safety topics with energy storage as its central contribution and offers one specific, testable claim that modellers of propagation in modules could use, the trigger-dependent correction to a lumped-calibrated heat share, and this outweighs what pulls toward a desk decline, namely an abstract that leans on its own defined terms, hedged conclusions that end on what the published data cannot rule out, fitted trigger energies mostly above calorimetry, a delivery time taken from two records and a threshold comparison resting on six tests, all of which are for reviewers to weigh rather than grounds to decline without review.
