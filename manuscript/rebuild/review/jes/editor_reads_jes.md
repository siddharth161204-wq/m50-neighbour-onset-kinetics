## Read 20

> The paper models sixteen published tests on pairs of touching LG M50 cells, using NCM811/graphite decomposition kinetics, to ask how much heat a cell in thermal runaway must deliver to its neighbour for the neighbour to follow, whether that answer changes when the neighbour is treated as a single temperature (lumped) or as a three-dimensional temperature field (resolved), and whether the published tests can tell. It finds that, whether the failing cell's energy is fitted to measured peak temperatures (31.6 to 54.3 kJ in fourteen strong-trigger tests, mostly above the 33.2 kJ maximum from calorimetry, and 9.0 and 16.6 kJ in two weak ones) or held at that maximum, conduction and radiation alone run the resolved neighbour away in no test, that the eight strong-trigger tests that propagated need 4.45 to 7.54 kJ delivered to the neighbour's facing side over 21.4 s if the neighbour is resolved and 0.26 to 5.96 kJ if it is lumped, that at nominal inputs the resolved threshold is at least 1.36 times the lumped one after the four strong triggers searched in three dimensions but at most 0.91 times after both weak ones, so that a neighbour's share of vented heat calibrated with a lumped model may need a correction whose direction follows the trigger's energy, and that the published inputs rule out no heat path delivering 27.9 kJ within 21.4 s, leaving a facing-side heat-flux measurement as the proposed test of these needs. I would send it to review, though not by a wide margin, and what decides it is that on these texts it clears the journal's formal bars, sits squarely within its listed topics of modelling and safety of energy storage systems, and tests a well-posed question, one that bears directly on how module propagation models are calibrated, against sixteen experiments rather than one, whereas the weaknesses that tempt a desk rejection (trigger energies fitted to peak temperatures the author suspects are flame-inflated, a single 21.4 s delivery time taken from two records, a closing finding that the published data rule out no heat path, no explicit claim of what is new relative to earlier propagation studies, and dense prose built on self-defined terms that makes the abstract and highlights hard to follow) are for reviewers to weigh rather than reasons to decline it unseen.

## Read 21

> The paper models sixteen published tests in which one of two touching LG M50 cells is driven into thermal runaway, using NCM811/graphite decomposition kinetics, to ask how much heat must reach the neighbour's facing side for it to follow by the measured time, how that answer changes when the neighbour is treated as one temperature (lumped) rather than as a three-dimensional temperature field (resolved), and whether the published records can say which heat path supplies that heat. It finds that conduction and radiation alone never run the resolved neighbour away, whether the failing cell's energy is fitted to its peak temperature, which puts most fits above the 33.2 kJ calorimetric maximum, or capped at that value; that with heat delivered over 21.4 s the eight propagating strong-trigger tests need 4.45 to 7.54 kJ with a resolved neighbour but 0.26 to 5.96 kJ with a lumped one; that the resolved threshold is at least 1.36 times the lumped one after four strong triggers but at most 0.91 times it after the two weak ones, so a vent-heat share calibrated in a lumped model may need a trigger-dependent correction in a resolved one; and that published inputs rule out no path delivering 27.9 kJ to the facing side within 21.4 s, leaving a facing-side heat-flux measurement as the proposed test. I would send it to review, though not by a wide margin, and what decides it is that the paper sits squarely within the journal's modelling and safety topics and puts specific, checkable numbers, anchored to sixteen tests, on an assumption that module-level propagation models routinely make, whereas the things that pull towards declining, namely fitted trigger energies that mostly exceed the calorimetric maximum with only a tentative explanation, a lumped-versus-resolved direction resting on four strong and two weak tests, a closing result that the data cannot tell heat paths apart, and an abstract whose coined, compressed phrasing makes the reader work for the result, are weaknesses for reviewers to test rather than reasons to decline it without review.

## Read 22

> The paper asks how much heat a lithium-ion cell in thermal runaway must deliver to a touching neighbour for the neighbour to follow, and whether the answer changes when the neighbour is modelled as a single temperature (lumped) or as a three-dimensional temperature field (resolved), using NCM811/graphite decomposition kinetics to model sixteen published propagation tests on pairs of LG M50 cells. It finds that conduction and radiation alone run no resolved neighbour away, whether each trigger's energy is fitted to its measured peak temperature, which mostly gives values above the 33.2 kJ calorimetric maximum, or held to that maximum; that the eight propagating strong-trigger tests need 4.45 to 7.54 kJ delivered to the facing side over 21.4 s if the neighbour is resolved but 0.26 to 5.96 kJ if lumped; and that at nominal inputs the resolved threshold is at least 1.36 times the lumped one after the four strong triggers searched in three dimensions but at most 0.91 times after both weak ones, from which it infers that a vent-heat share calibrated in a lumped model may need a correction whose direction depends on the trigger's energy, while conceding that published inputs rule out no path delivering as much as 27.9 kJ in that time. I would send it to review, though narrowly: what decides it is that the work sits squarely in the journal's modelling and safety topics with energy storage as its central contribution and offers one specific, testable claim that modellers of propagation in modules could use, the trigger-dependent correction to a lumped-calibrated heat share, and this outweighs what pulls toward a desk decline, namely an abstract that leans on its own defined terms, hedged conclusions that end on what the published data cannot rule out, fitted trigger energies mostly above calorimetry, a delivery time taken from two records and a threshold comparison resting on six tests, all of which are for reviewers to weigh rather than grounds to decline without review.

## What changed after them

Nothing. All three would send the paper to review, each narrowly. None took a sentence to say something the paper does not say. Each restates the findings as the texts give them, keeping the hedges ("at nominal inputs", "may need", "perhaps", "rule out no path"). What each weighed against the paper is either what wording cannot change in it: fitted energies above calorimetry with a suspected cause, one delivery time from two records, a direction resting on six tests, and bounds that exclude no path. Or it is the density of the abstract's defined terms (all three). Read 20 also notes that no claim of what is new is made, which the brief's rules exclude ("first", "novel", "not previously").
