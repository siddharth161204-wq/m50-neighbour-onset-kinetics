The paper models sixteen published tests of thermal runaway propagation between two touching LG M50 cells, using NCM811/graphite kinetics fitted to calorimetry and representing the neighbour either as one lumped temperature or as a resolved three-dimensional field, to ask how much heat must reach the neighbour through its facing side for it to follow the failing cell, and whether published tests can tell. It finds that conduction and radiation from a failing cell capped at calorimetry's 33.2 kJ leave the neighbour at least 29 K short of runaway, that reproducing the eight strong-trigger propagations takes 4.45 to 7.54 kJ through the facing side if the neighbour is resolved and 0.26 to 5.96 kJ if lumped, under a tenth of the 76.74 kJ median ejecta energy, that the resolved threshold is at least 1.36 times the lumped one after four strong triggers but at most 0.91 times after the weak ones, and that published data rule out no delivery path, so it proposes a heat-flux measurement to test these requirements. I would decline it without review, and what decides it is that the headline heat requirements come from fitting rather than from a test against an independent observation: each failing cell's energy is fitted to its own peak temperature, reaching up to 54.3 kJ against the 33.2 kJ calorimetric ceiling on which the 29 K shortfall rests, a gap none of the four texts explains, and the neighbour's heat, which the paper says no published test measures, is then chosen to match each runaway time, so the work offers a model-dependent range and a proposed experiment rather than a validated result, and it never says what it adds to existing propagation models.
