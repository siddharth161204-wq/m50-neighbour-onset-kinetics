The paper models sixteen published tests in which one of two touching LG M50 cells was driven into thermal runaway, using NCM811/graphite kinetics fitted to calorimetry and treating the neighbour either as one lumped temperature or as a resolved three-dimensional field, to work out how much heat must reach the neighbour through its facing side for it to follow and whether published data can say what delivers that heat. It finds that conduction and radiation alone leave the neighbour at least 29 K short of runaway even from a failing cell releasing 33.2 kJ, the most calorimetry has measured for this cell; that matching the eight propagating strong-trigger tests takes only 4.45 to 7.54 kJ through the facing side over about 21 s with the neighbour resolved, or 0.26 to 5.96 kJ with it lumped, which is under a tenth of the cell's median ejecta energy; that the resolved neighbour needs at least 1.36 times the lumped one's energy to run away after the four strong triggers it searched in three dimensions but at most 0.91 times after the two weak ones, so lumping errs in opposite directions depending on the trigger; and that published inputs rule out none of the paths that might deliver this heat, so it proposes a heat-flux measurement on the facing side as the test. I would send it to review, but only just: what decides it is that it takes two choices every propagation model must make, how much vented heat reaches the neighbour and whether to lump the cell, and tests both against sixteen experiments, giving numbers, open code, predictions committed in advance and a named measurement that could falsify them, all squarely in the journal's safety scope, whereas what nearly made me decline is that the abstract and highlights read as a run of ranges that never say what existing propagation models get wrong, lean on undefined sources such as the Databank and the two published records, and leave unexplained why fitted failing-cell energies of up to 54.3 kJ exceed the 33.2 kJ calorimetric maximum used as the bound.
