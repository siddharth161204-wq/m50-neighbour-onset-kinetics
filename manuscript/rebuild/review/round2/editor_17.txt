The paper uses NCM811/graphite kinetics to model thermal runaway propagation between two touching LG M50 cells against sixteen published two-cell tests, solving the neighbour both as one lumped temperature and as a three-dimensional field, to estimate how much heat must enter the neighbour's facing side for it to run away and whether published inputs can constrain the paths that could deliver that heat. Fitting each failing cell's energy to its measured peak temperature gives 31.6 to 54.3 kJ in the fourteen strong-trigger tests, mostly above the 33.2 kJ calorimetric maximum for this cell, and 9.0 and 16.6 kJ in the two weak-trigger tests; conduction and radiation alone never trigger runaway in the resolved neighbour; matching the onset times of the eight propagating strong-trigger tests takes 4.45 to 7.54 kJ through the facing side over 21.4 s for the resolved neighbour but 0.26 to 5.96 kJ for the lumped one; the resolved runaway threshold is at least 1.36 times the lumped one after four strong triggers and at most 0.91 times after the two weak ones, so a heat share calibrated in one representation needs a trigger-dependent correction before it can serve the other; and published inputs rule out no path that would deliver 27.9 kJ to the facing side within 21.4 s, which leaves a facing-side heat-flux measurement as the proposed test. I would decline it without review, and what decides it is that these four texts, in phrasing dense enough that I had to decode it, show no advance that is both general and well founded: its bounding of the heat paths from published inputs excludes none of them, and its most transferable result, a trigger-dependent factor between lumped and resolved thresholds given only as bounds, rests on four strong-trigger and two weak-trigger searches of one cell type in one touching configuration and on failing-cell energies mostly above the calorimetric maximum, while neither the abstract nor the letter says what the work adds to existing propagation models.
