The paper asks how much heat a touching neighbour must receive through its facing side to follow a failing LG M50 cell into thermal runaway, and whether published experiments can say how that heat arrives, by modelling sixteen published two-cell tests with NCM811/graphite kinetics, fitting each failing cell's energy to its measured peak temperature, and solving the neighbour both as a single lumped temperature and as a resolved three-dimensional field. It finds that conduction and radiation alone never drive the resolved neighbour into runaway, that matching the measured runaway times of the eight propagating strong-trigger tests takes 4.45 to 7.54 kJ through the facing side over 21.4 s with a resolved neighbour but 0.26 to 5.96 kJ with a lumped one, that the resolved neighbour's threshold for runaway at all is at least 1.36 times the lumped one's after four strong triggers but at most 0.91 times after the two weak ones, so a heat share calibrated in one representation needs a trigger-dependent correction in the other, and that published inputs rule out none of the candidate heat paths, leaving a facing-side heat-flux measurement as the proposed test. I would decline it without review, and what decides it is that these texts promise no conclusive or general result, since by the paper's own account the published tests cannot say which path delivers the heat, its main transferable claim rests on six cases and is buried in a number-dense abstract, and the failing-cell fits that the neighbour results build on mostly exceed the 33.2 kJ calorimetric maximum with no explanation in the abstract or cover letter, which leaves a model-to-model sensitivity study of a single cell type that the open archive and pre-registered predictions, welcome as they are, do not lift over the bar for review at this journal.
