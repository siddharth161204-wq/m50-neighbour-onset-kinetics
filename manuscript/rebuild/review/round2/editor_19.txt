The paper models thermal runaway propagation between two touching LG M50 cells with NCM811/graphite kinetics against sixteen published two-cell tests, treating the neighbour either as one lumped temperature or as a resolved three-dimensional field, to find how much heat must reach the neighbour through its facing side for it to run away and whether published data can constrain that heat. It finds that failing-cell energies fitted to peak temperatures mostly exceed the 33.2 kJ calorimetric maximum after strong triggers, that conduction and radiation alone never run the resolved neighbour away, that the eight propagating strong-trigger tests need 4.45 to 7.54 kJ through the facing side over an assumed 21.4 s if the neighbour is resolved but 0.26 to 5.96 kJ if it is lumped, that the resolved threshold is at least 1.36 times the lumped one after the four strong triggers searched in three dimensions but at most 0.91 times after both weak ones, so a heat share calibrated in one representation cannot serve the other without a trigger-dependent correction, and that published inputs rule out none of the paths that could deliver the heat. I would decline it without review, and what decides it is that these texts do not establish a significant advance: the work covers one cell type in pairs, leaves the delivery path open, rests its required heat on a delivery time read from two records and on failing-cell energies above the calorimetric maximum that go unexplained, and is not positioned against any prior propagation model, so the one result that could justify review, a factor between the two representations that reverses direction with trigger strength, stands on six three-dimensional searches, is given no physical explanation and is stated too densely to judge on a first read.
