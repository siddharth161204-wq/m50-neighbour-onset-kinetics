Title:
Thermal runaway propagation between two lithium-ion cells: resolved and lumped neighbour models against sixteen tests

Abstract:
Propagation models must assume the heat a failing cell's neighbour receives, unmeasured in published tests, and whether each cell is one temperature. Sixteen tests of touching LG M50 pairs are modelled both ways, lumped or resolved in three dimensions, with NCM811/graphite kinetics. Fitted to peak temperatures, failing-cell energies are 31.6 to 54.3 kJ in fourteen (strong triggers), mostly above calorimetry's 33.2 kJ maximum, and 9.0 and 16.6 kJ in two (weak); conduction and radiation run no resolved neighbour away at either energy. Over 21.4 s, the mean of the two rise times read from the two published records, 4.45 to 7.54 kJ through the facing side runs the resolved neighbour away when the eight propagating strong-trigger tests did, and 0.26 to 5.96 kJ the lumped one. To run away at all, the resolved neighbour needs at least 1.36 times the lumped one's energy after four strong triggers, at most 0.91 times after both weak ones, searched in three dimensions, so here a heat share calibrated with one representation needs a trigger-dependent correction for the other. Published inputs rule out no path delivering 27.9 kJ within 21.4 s; a heat flux measured on the facing side could test the needs.

Dear Dr Belharouak,

I submit the manuscript "Thermal runaway propagation between two lithium-ion cells: resolved and lumped neighbour models against sixteen tests" for consideration as a research article in the Journal of Power Sources.

Models of thermal runaway propagation in modules must choose how much of a failing cell's vented heat reaches its neighbour, which published tests do not measure, and must represent each cell as one temperature or as a resolved temperature field. The manuscript asks how much heat must reach a neighbour for it to follow, and whether published tests can say, for two touching LG M50 cells modelled with kinetics fitted to calorimetry of NCM811 and graphite, against sixteen published two-cell tests.

Each failing cell's energy is fitted to its own measured peak temperature, 31.6 to 54.3 kJ in fourteen tests (strong triggers), mostly above the 33.2 kJ that calorimetry measured at most for this cell, and 9.0 and 16.6 kJ in two (weak), and the neighbour is solved both as one temperature and as a three-dimensional field; in each, the heat it needs through its side facing the failing cell to run away at the test's measured time is searched, and the bounds that published inputs set on each path that could deliver it are worked out.

At either energy, conduction and radiation run no resolved neighbour away, so further heat must reach it. With heat delivered over 21.4 s, the mean of the two rise times read from the two published records, the eight tests that propagated after strong triggers need 4.45 to 7.54 kJ if the neighbour is resolved in three dimensions and 0.26 to 5.96 kJ if it is lumped. To run away at all, the resolved neighbour needs at least 1.36 times the lumped neighbour's energy after the four strong triggers searched in three dimensions and at most 0.91 times after both weak ones, so in these tests a heat share calibrated with one representation needs a correction that depends on the trigger before it can serve the other. Published inputs rule out no path delivering 27.9 kJ to the facing side within 21.4 s.

For readers who model propagation in packs, the paper gives the heat a neighbour needs under each of the two common representations of a cell, set against sixteen tests, the factor between them and its direction, and the measurement, a heat flux on the neighbour's facing side, that could test them.

It falls within the journal's priority area of safety, as a computational study set against published experiments.

The code, inputs and run records are archived openly, with the study's 109 predictions, each committed before its runs.
